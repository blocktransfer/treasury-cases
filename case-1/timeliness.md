Yes. I found an IRS rule that is unusually close to what you're describing. It says that a taxpayer's credible firsthand testimony shouldn't simply be disregarded because it benefits the taxpayer.

## 1. IRS Internal Revenue Manual §4.10.7.3.2 — Oral Testimony

The IRS's own instructions to examiners state:

> "uncontradicted statements which are not improbable or unreasonable should not be disregarded."


- [4.10.7 Issue Resolution](https://www.irs.gov/irm/part4/irm_04-010-007)

The same provision recognizes that oral testimony may be the only available evidence when records do not exist. It also directs examiners to evaluate credibility and surrounding circumstances, and permits affidavits when documentary evidence is unavailable.

This is directly relevant to your situation:

- You personally witnessed the USPS employee hand-cancel the envelope on March 16, 2026.
- You have consistently maintained that date.
- You no longer possess the postmarked envelope.
- You have already explained the circumstances in a signed Form 843.

Important limitation: This is IRS internal examination guidance, not a statute requiring the IRS to believe every taxpayer. It supports considering your account, but does not automatically establish the mailing date under IRC §7502.

## 2. IRC §7803(a)(3) — Taxpayer Bill of Rights

Congress expressly recognizes:

- §7803(a)(3)(D): The right to challenge the IRS's position and be heard.
- §7803(a)(3)(J): The right to a fair and just tax system.

The statute directs the IRS Commissioner to ensure employees act consistently with those rights.


- [U.S. Code](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=%28title%3A26+section%3A7803+edition%3Aprelim%29)
- [Taxpayer Bill of Rights](https://www.irs.gov/taxpayer-bill-of-rights)

These provisions provide a legal basis for requesting a fair assessment of your account rather than an automatic rejection, although they don't remove applicable evidentiary requirements.

## 3. 28 USC §1746 — Statements under penalty of perjury

This law allows a signed, dated declaration under penalty of perjury to serve in place of a sworn affidavit in many federal proceedings.


- [U.S.C. Title 28](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title28/html/USCODE-2024-title28-partV-chap115-sec1746.htm)

You could submit a separate declaration describing exactly what you witnessed at the post office, ending with:

> I declare under penalty of perjury that the foregoing is true and correct.

That gives your firsthand account a formal evidentiary form. It does not, by itself, satisfy every requirement for proving a USPS postmark.

## 4. IRC §6699(a) — Reasonable cause

This statute expressly allows relief from an S corporation late-filing penalty when the failure was due to reasonable cause. It does not require eligibility for First Time Abate.

## 5. Research point — 26 USC § 7491(a) and the burden of proof

[26 USC § 7491(a)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t26/s7491/) provides that in a qualifying **court proceeding**, the burden of proof on a factual issue relevant to liability for a tax imposed by Subtitle A or B may shift to the IRS when the taxpayer introduces credible evidence. The shift is conditional: the taxpayer must satisfy applicable substantiation requirements, maintain records **actually required** by federal tax law, and cooperate with reasonable IRS requests. A corporation must also meet the eligibility criteria incorporated from [26 USC § 7430(c)(4)(A)(ii)](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t26/s7430/), and another specific statutory burden-of-proof rule may control instead.

**Questions for further research in this case:**

- Could credible, signed firsthand testimony about the March 16, 2026 USPS hand-cancellation support a contested factual finding, and what corroboration could strengthen it? Distinguish credibility of testimony from the specific proof-of-mailing rules under [26 USC § 7502](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t26/s7502/) and its regulations.
- Does any law actually require the taxpayer to *retain* a receipt or photograph of the postmark, as opposed to potentially needing evidence of timely mailing to prevail in a dispute? Section 7491(a)(2)(B) refers to records that are **required**; it does not itself create a new postmark-receipt retention obligation.
- Is § 7491(a) applicable at all to the disputed [26 USC § 6699](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t26/s6699/) **S corporation late-filing penalty**? Section 6699 is in Subtitle F, whereas § 7491(a) expressly addresses taxes imposed by Subtitles A and B. Do **not** assert a burden shift for this penalty without separate supporting authority.
- Distinguish the possible use of § 7491(a) in later tax litigation from the present IRS correspondence and administrative appeals. Also note that § 7491(c)'s penalty-related burden of production expressly concerns **individuals**, whereas this penalty is assessed against a corporation. Research the actual burden and available defenses, including reasonable cause under § 6699(a).
