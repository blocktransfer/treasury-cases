# Appellate access for self-represented and indigent petitioners

*Research note — Supreme Court certiorari practice; updated November 7, 2026.*

## Question and conclusion

What support do Justice John Paul Stevens's dissents provide for an argument against barriers to Supreme Court review for a self-represented litigant who cannot afford a filing fee?

Stevens argued that the Court should normally deny meritless petitions on their own terms, rather than impose prospective financial barriers that might prevent a future colorable petition from being considered. This is a persuasive **dissenting** argument about access to justice, not a controlling holding invalidating filing fees or sanctions. The majority maintained that repeat frivolous filings justify measures to conserve resources for other litigants.

The right to represent oneself and permission to proceed *in forma pauperis* (IFP, without prepaying docket fees) are distinct questions. [28 USC § 1654](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-134/t28/s1654/) permits parties to conduct their own cases personally or through counsel, subject to court rules; it does not itself dispense with filing fees, entitle a litigant to certiorari, or allow a nonlawyer to represent someone else.

## Justice Stevens's dissent in *Zatko v. California* (1991)

In *Zatko v. California*, 502 U.S. 16, 18–20 (1991), Stevens dissented, joined by Justice Harry Blackmun, when the Court first applied newly adopted Supreme Court Rule 39.8 to deny IFP status to Vladimir Zatko and James L. Martin.

Stevens observed that, between the rule's July 1, 1991 effective date and the November 4, 1991 decision, indigent litigants had submitted **almost 1,000 petitions** that the Court denied by ordinary procedures. In **his assessment**, well over half could have been classified as frivolous, yet the Court had resolved them without determining whether Rule 39.8 applied. He regarded this as evidence that routine denials were workable without specially burdening the petitioners.

His central concern was forward-looking: a petitioner's history of frivolous filings did not establish that all *future* petitions would lack colorable merit. He wrote:

> By its action today, the Court places yet another barrier in the way of indigent petitioners.

Stevens argued that singling out poor petitioners conveyed an unfortunate message about equal access for wealthy and indigent litigants. He preferred denying the particular petitions rather than withholding IFP permission. See [*Zatko*, Stevens dissent, 502 U.S. at 18–20](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/502/16).

## The majority's opposing rationale

The *Zatko* majority viewed Rule 39.8 as a targeted response to serious repeated abuse, not a general restriction on the poor. It recorded **73 petitions by Zatko over 10 years (34 over two years)** and **more than 45 by Martin over 10 years (15 over two years)**, and concluded that repetitious frivolous submissions diverted judicial resources. The Court denied IFP permission for the cases at hand but allowed payment of the ordinary docket fee. See [*Zatko*, 502 U.S. at 16–18](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/502/16).

## The later prospective restriction in *Martin* (1992)

In *Martin v. District of Columbia Court of Appeals*, 506 U.S. 1 (1992), the Court went further, directing its Clerk not to accept **future noncriminal certiorari petitions** from Martin without payment of the docketing fee and compliance with the filing rules. It expressly excluded criminal matters from this prospective restriction.

Stevens, again joined by Blackmun, dissented at pages 4–5. He reasoned that simply denying Martin's petitions would be a more efficient use of Court resources than drafting, entering, and policing a prospective filing restriction, while avoiding damage to the Court's tradition of open access. He reaffirmed his dissents in *Zatko* and earlier related cases. See [*Martin*, 506 U.S. 1, 4–5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/506/1).

## Reaffirmation in *Stephen v. IRS* (2008)

The [Supreme Court order list of May 12, 2008, page 13, docket 07-9980](https://www.supremecourt.gov/orders/courtorders/051208pzr.pdf#page=13) denied Jimmie Stephen IFP status, dismissed his petition under Rule 39.8, and instructed the Clerk to refuse future **noncriminal** petitions absent the required fee and compliant filings. Stevens dissented by reference to his earlier views in *Martin*, 506 U.S. at 4. The 2008 order contains **no separate written explanation** by Stevens.

## Relevance to an appellate-access argument

A properly framed argument may cite Stevens's dissents for these limited propositions:

1. A court can reject an unmeritorious petition without automatically treating its author as incapable of presenting a later meritorious claim.
2. Prospective financial barriers may fall especially hard on people unable to pay, including self-represented litigants.
3. Equal access and the risk of deterring future colorable claims are considerations when evaluating discretionary filing restrictions.

**Limits:** These propositions represent Stevens's position, **not the Supreme Court majority's rule**. They do **not** establish an unconditional constitutional right to free filings, unrestricted repetitive petitions, or discretionary certiorari review. Self-representation under 28 USC § 1654 does not resolve IFP eligibility.

As of the [Supreme Court Rules effective March 16, 2026](https://www.supremecourt.gov/filingandrules/2026RulesoftheCourt_WEB.pdf), Rule 38 prescribes a docketing fee for ordinary petitions and Rule 39 provides a process for seeking IFP status. Rule 39.8 permits denial of IFP status when a petition is frivolous or malicious; Rule 39 also acknowledges documents submitted by a *pro se* party. An appellate argument should identify the precise court, filing rule, litigation posture, and record before invoking the dissent.
