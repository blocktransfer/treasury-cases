# Appellate-access references by procedural stage

This directory indexes the primary authorities supporting [the research note on Justice Stevens's dissents](../stevens-indigent-petitioners.md). The order follows **the procedural issue the source addresses**, from filing a petition to the Court's disposition and any later filing restriction. It is **not** a docket chronology for one lawsuit: *Zatko*, *Martin*, and *Stephen* are separate proceedings, and these references concern certiorari/filing motions rather than an oral merits hearing.

## 1. Before review: appearance, petition filing, and fees

- [28 USC § 1654 — appearance personally or by counsel](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/119-134/t28/s1654/): governs appearing personally or through counsel, subject to court rules. It does not waive filing fees or confer a right to certiorari.
- [Supreme Court Rules effective March 16, 2026](https://www.supremecourt.gov/filingandrules/2026RulesoftheCourt_WEB.pdf): Rule 12 covers the petition process; Rule 33 addresses document preparation; Rule 38 specifies fees; Rule 39 governs an application to proceed *in forma pauperis* (IFP). These **current** rules are useful for present-day filing practice, not as a substitute for the historical rules applied in 1991–1992.

## 2. Screening a pending petition: alleged frivolousness and IFP eligibility

- [*Zatko v. California*, 502 U.S. 16, 16–20 (1991)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/502/16) ([CourtListener opinion](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112656/zatko-v-california/)): the Court invoked newly adopted Rule 39.8 to deny IFP status on pending petitions by repeat filers; Stevens, joined by Blackmun, dissented at pages 18–20. The dissent distinguished denying particular unmeritorious petitions from imposing barriers that might affect later, colorable petitions. The majority's application of Rule 39.8—not the dissent—is controlling.
- [Current Rule 39.8, at page 56 of the Court's 2026 Rules](https://www.supremecourt.gov/filingandrules/2026RulesoftheCourt_WEB.pdf#page=60): permits denial of IFP leave when a petition is frivolous or malicious. This is a current procedural reference; read *Zatko* for the Court's 1991 application.

## 3. Disposition: the petition-stage order and prospective restrictions

- [*Martin v. District of Columbia Court of Appeals*, 506 U.S. 1, 1–5 (1992)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/506/1) ([CourtListener opinion](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/112790/martin-v-district-of-columbia-court-of-appeals/)): the Court denied Martin IFP leave on his pending petitions, set a deadline to pay fees and comply with the filing rules, and directed the Clerk not to accept **future noncriminal certiorari petitions** from him without the required fee and compliant papers. Stevens, joined by Blackmun, dissented at pages 4–5, arguing for disposition of individual petitions instead of policing a prospective order. The restriction was the Court's order, not a ruling on the underlying substantive claims.

## 4. After disposition: later application of the restriction

- [May 12, 2008 Supreme Court order list, page 13, *Stephen v. IRS*, No. 07-9980](https://www.supremecourt.gov/orders/courtorders/051208pzr.pdf#page=13): the Court denied IFP status, dismissed Stephen's certiorari petition under Rule 39.8, and imposed a prospective fee-and-format requirement for future noncriminal petitions. Stevens dissented by citation to *Martin* at page 4; **the order includes no separate Stevens opinion**. This is a later, separate example of the practice, not a subsequent stage in Martin's docket.

## Use and limits

The progression is **filing / eligibility → Rule 39.8 screening → disposition and any prospective order → later application in other cases**. These authorities address access to Supreme Court review, not proof of a hearing or an entitlement to a decision on the merits. Keep the majority's rulings distinct from Stevens's noncontrolling dissents.
