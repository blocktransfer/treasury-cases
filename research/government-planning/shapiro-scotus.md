# Shapiro v. United States (1948): Congressional Price Controls and Required Price Records

## Question and historical setting

Why did the federal government require a private fruit-and-produce wholesaler to keep records of the prices he charged in United States dollars, and why could those records later be compelled in a criminal investigation? The relevant chain of authority runs from the **Emergency Price Control Act of 1942**, through **Office of Price Administration (OPA)** regulations specifying dollar-and-cent ceilings and documentation, to the decision in [Shapiro v. United States, 335 U.S. 1 (1948)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/104585/shapiro-v-united-states/).

**Central distinction:** Congress did **not** order every merchant to calculate an index of inflation or measure the changing purchasing power of the dollar. Congress authorized control of the *nominal prices of commodities sold in dollars*, and authorized regulations requiring merchants to retain evidence of historical and current transaction prices and the method used to determine each lawful ceiling. Comparisons of a charged price with a benchmark or ceiling made evasion detectable. The legal duty to maintain particular books came from the applicable regulation, not simply from Congress announcing an abstract economic objective.

The relevant controls were temporary wartime rules, not a statement of present-day price-control or recordkeeping law.

## 1. Congressional record: why Congress sought to restrain consumer prices

### July 30, 1941: President Roosevelt's request

President Franklin D. Roosevelt asked Congress to legislate against wartime inflation, observing that higher defense spending and civilian demand competed for a limited supply of goods. His message explained that an increased dollar price could allocate scarce commodities to bidders without increasing their production; higher living costs would especially burden persons living on fixed incomes. This is the policy origin of the price-control legislation, **not itself an enforceable merchant recordkeeping mandate**.

Primary source: [Message to Congress on Price Control Legislation (July 30, 1941)](https://www.presidency.ucsb.edu/documents/message-congress-price-control-legislation).

### November 7, 1941: House Report 77-1409 accompanying H.R. 5990

The House Committee on Banking and Currency reported the proposed **Emergency Price Control Act of 1941**, H.R. 5990, in [H. Rept. 77-1409](https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/SERIALSET-10558_00_00-043-1409-0000) ([official scanned report](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/SERIALSET-10558_00_00-043-1409-0000/pdf/SERIALSET-10558_00_00-043-1409-0000.pdf)). The report documents the proposed powers and their rationale, including:

- **Commodity prices (report pp. 4–5):** the Administrator could prescribe generally fair and equitable maximum prices. The report discussed consideration of prices around October 1, 1941, costs of production and transportation, and profits. Specific ceilings could depend on the commodity, seller, transaction, and circumstances.
- **Forms of ceilings (report pp. 5–6):** the committee recognized that enforceable ceilings might take the form of dollar prices, margins, commissions, fees, differentials, or allowances. Comparing a present sale with the ceiling therefore required more than recording a single sticker price in isolation.
- **Obtaining information (report p. 9):** the committee expressly described authority to investigate, inspect inventories and documents, require the keeping of records, and require reports. The rationale was both the formulation *and enforcement* of price restrictions. The report also addressed compulsory process, immunity, and confidentiality.

This report describes a **committee bill**; its language must not be treated as the final enacted text without checking subsequent House and Senate amendments.

### House revisions, Senate restoration, and the January 1942 debate

The Supreme Court later reconstructed an important legislative dispute in [Shapiro, 335 U.S. at 8–16](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/104585/shapiro-v-united-states/). The House considered Representative Wolcott's November 28, 1941 amendments, which narrowed investigatory authority and removed broader provisions for mandatory record maintenance. Following the December 1941 declaration of war, Senate consideration restored substantial powers for recordkeeping, reports, inspection, and licensing. The Court treated that legislative sequence as evidence that access to records was meant to uncover violations, not merely to inform future ceilings.

The [Congressional Record, 88 Cong. Rec. 88 et seq. (Senate, Jan. 8, 1942)](https://www.congress.gov/77/crecb/1942/01/08/GPO-CRECB-1942-pt1-4.pdf), records deliberations on H.R. 5990 and opposing perspectives. Around pp. 110–111, Senator W. Lee O'Daniel criticized the proposed controls and contrasted an asserted increase in Washington retail beefsteak prices from **45 cents to 75 cents per pound** with different developments in cattle prices. That is a **senator's illustrative claim during debate**, not an independently audited national price series. It demonstrates that legislators debated changes in *dollar-and-cent prices at different stages of distribution* and disagreed about how far regulation should reach. Congress's enacted authority, rather than any individual speech, establishes the legal duties.

The official [U.S. Capitol Visitor Center's H.R. 5990 archival description](https://www.visitthecapitol.gov/artifact/hr-5990-act-further-national-defense-and-security-checking-inflationary-tendencies) identifies the enrolled January 27, 1942 bill and explains its intended consumer-goods coverage (including food, tires, sugar, coffee, shoes, cars, and bicycles). Coverage nevertheless varied by specific regulation and date.

## 2. Congress's enacted commands: Public Law 77-421, January 30, 1942

The [Emergency Price Control Act of 1942, ch. 26, 56 Stat. 23](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-56/pdf/STATUTE-56-Pg23-2.pdf), became law on January 30, 1942. Its operative provisions distinguish **policy**, **price ceilings**, **recordkeeping authority**, and **enforcement**:

| Provision | Authority or obligation | Relevance to dollar-price evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Section 1(a), 56 Stat. 23–24** | Stated purposes: stabilize prices, prevent abnormal increases and profiteering, protect consumers and persons with limited or fixed incomes, and avoid waste of defense appropriations. | Explains why the government wanted to check increases in the dollar price of goods; does not by itself designate particular ledgers. |
| **Section 2(a), 56 Stat. 24–25** | Allowed maximum commodity prices when prices rose or threatened to rise inconsistently with statutory purposes, subject to standards including fair and equitable treatment. In determining ceilings the Administrator was, *so far as practicable*, to consider prices prevailing **October 1–15, 1941**, or a representative alternate two-week period. | Congress prescribed an historical reference for agency price-setting, **not** a universal merchant sales-record baseline or a requirement to adjust prices for CPI. |
| **Section 4(a), 56 Stat. 28** | Prohibited transactions or acts violating operative regulations, orders, price schedules, and certain recordkeeping requirements. | Supplied the legal prohibition against charging beyond an applicable ceiling or violating applicable documentation rules. |
| **Section 202(a)–(c), 56 Stat. 30** | Authorized investigations and, by regulation or order, required information, records and documents, reports, inspection and copying, and subpoenas for testimony and documents. | The principal congressional delegation for compelled production and required books of price transactions. |
| **Section 202(g)–(h), 56 Stat. 30** | Addressed privilege against self-incrimination, incorporation of statutory immunity, and confidentiality of submitted information. | Produced the statutory-immunity controversy decided in *Shapiro*. |
| **Sections 203 and 205** | Supplied mechanisms to challenge regulations and for administration/enforcement. | A ceiling was legally reviewable and enforceable; compliance records could be evidence. |

The two historical dates should **not** be conflated: **October 1–15, 1941** was a statutory reference for an Administrator establishing a ceiling under § 2(a), whereas **March 1942** was the seller-specific base month selected in the later General Maximum Price Regulation. Their different functions matter.

For a precise statement of what Congress required, read § 202(b): the statute empowered the Administrator to require businesses to **make and keep records**, **make reports**, and **permit inspection and copying**. It did *not* impose a single universal form, spreadsheet, index, filing cadence, or list of commodities on all businesses. Those details had to be supplied by regulations or orders.

## 3. How businesses actually tracked dollar-price changes: General Maximum Price Regulation (1942)

The OPA's [General Maximum Price Regulation (GMPR), 7 Fed. Reg. 3153, 3330 (1942), as amended through December 16, 1942](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-PR32_4200-86ed02a3745247a8091a19c1fd4154fd/html/GOVPUB-PR32_4200-86ed02a3745247a8091a19c1fd4154fd.htm) supplies the concrete mechanics. The OPA also published [Bulletin No. 2, *What Every Retailer Should Know About the General Maximum Price Regulation* (May 1942)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-PR32_4200-b232786efb10ce36452a2265828e39ea/pdf/GOVPUB-PR32_4200-b232786efb10ce36452a2265828e39ea.pdf), particularly pp. 22–34.

### Step A: establish the reference selling price in dollars

Under GMPR § 1499.2, absent an applicable exception or more specific rule, a seller's ceiling generally started with the **highest price charged during March 1942** to the relevant class of purchaser for the same commodity or service. If that was unavailable, the regulation supplied an ordered method using a similar commodity or a comparable seller; § 1499.3 provided additional calculations for otherwise unpriceable goods. “Highest price charged” had detailed rules concerning deliveries, offers, customer classes, and customary differentials. This was **not** simply the average price or the current cost of replenishment.

The regulation made the **price in dollars and cents**, the **unit** (item, pound, crate, etc.), the **identity of the item**, and the **purchaser class** indispensable to a valid comparison. A nominal price of $5 per *crate* is not comparable to $5 per *pound*; a price for one grade or customer class might not control another.

### Step B: preserve the historical evidence

GMPR **§ 1499.11(a)** required covered sellers to preserve existing records showing prices charged or offered in **March 1942** for covered goods and services. Section **1499.11(b)** further required a statement, generally by **July 1, 1942**, identifying the highest historical prices, the commodities or services, and customary discounts, allowances, and price differentials. The statement was retained for examination during ordinary business hours. A covered nonretail seller asserting substantial injury from public inspection could instead file it with the OPA's appropriate field office under the regulation's confidentiality procedure.

These were records of the *historical nominal dollar price*, not calculations converting 1942 dollars into dollars of another year.

### Step C: document subsequent prices and the calculation of the ceiling

GMPR **§ 1499.12** required records of the customary kind relating to prices charged after the regulation's effective date, *plus* records showing as precisely as possible how the seller determined its applicable maximum price. For covered retail establishments, the regulation's principal effective date was **May 18, 1942** (with particular provisions and other sellers having distinct effective dates).

The OPA could therefore examine both sides of a compliance comparison:

1. **Benchmark:** the properly documented historical price or lawful calculated ceiling.
2. **Transaction:** the price actually demanded or received on a dated sale.
3. **Adjustments:** authorized freight, discounts, allowances, grades, quantities, purchaser classes, or any later regulatory amendment.
4. **Result:** whether the actual dollar charge exceeded the permitted ceiling on that transaction.

For example, a merchant whose lawful ceiling was **$1.00 per item** could sell at **$0.90** but ordinarily could not increase the charge to **$1.10** absent legal authority to change the ceiling. This example is hypothetical and does not assert that any Shapiro transaction involved those amounts.

### Step D: mark, file, and update selected retail ceilings

GMPR **§ 1499.13** made some documentation visible to consumers. For designated **cost-of-living commodities**, retailers had to mark or post a maximum price visibly, in the format **“Ceiling Price $…”** or **“Our Ceiling $…”**. They generally had to file a statement of maximum prices with the local War Price and Rationing Board by July 1, 1942, and file monthly additions for *newly offered* designated goods, on the **tenth day of the following month**. This did **not** mean every transaction or every merchant's prices had to be filed monthly.

GMPR **§ 1499.14** required customary sales slips to continue; a purchaser could request a receipt identifying the date, seller, commodity or service, and **price received**. These receipts gave buyers—and investigators—a way to check the nominal dollar amount actually paid.

### Step E: record computations when March comparators were unavailable

For certain products without a usable March 1942 comparator, **GMPR § 1499.3(a)** and **Appendix A (Form GMPR:2)** supplied a calculation using a comparable commodity's maximum-price-to-replacement-cost ratio. A qualifying seller had to report the computed ceiling to the appropriate OPA field office **within ten days** of its determination.

The official form itself gives a numerical illustration: comparable commodity replacement cost **$4.00 per unit**, ceiling **$5.00 per unit**, ratio **125%**. If a qualifying new item cost **$6.00 per unit**, application of that same ratio would yield **$7.50 per unit** as a *hypothetical* calculated ceiling, subject to the regulation and possible OPA adjustment. This is an example of a legally prescribed **dollar-price calculation**, not of a currency-exchange rate, purchasing-power adjustment, or independent claim that all merchants could apply a 25% markup.

Sources: GMPR §§ 1499.2–1499.3, 1499.11–1499.14, 1499.24 (Appendix A); [official amended regulation and form](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-PR32_4200-86ed02a3745247a8091a19c1fd4154fd/html/GOVPUB-PR32_4200-86ed02a3745247a8091a19c1fd4154fd.htm).

## 4. The rule directly involved in Shapiro: Maximum Price Regulation No. 426

*Shapiro* is **not principally a case about a general retailer's March 1942 base-price book**. The petitioner was a **fruit-and-produce wholesaler** whose records were governed by specific OPA regulations, including **Maximum Price Regulations Nos. 271 and 426**. In the Supreme Court's account, OPA enforcement attorneys subpoenaed him on **September 29, 1944**, for **duplicate sales invoices, sales books, ledgers, inventory records, contracts, and sales records from September 1–28, 1944**. The underlying prosecution involved prohibited **tie-in sales**; the litigated question was whether production of required records yielded the statutory immunity he asserted. See [335 U.S. at 3–6](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/104585/shapiro-v-united-states/).

[Maximum Price Regulation No. 426, 8 Fed. Reg. 9546 (1943)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/104585/shapiro-v-united-states/) controlled covered **fresh fruits and vegetables for table use, except retail sales**. Its later consolidated text appears at [8 Fed. Reg. 16409 et seq. (Dec. 7, 1943)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1943-12-07/pdf/FR-1943-12-07.pdf). Different rules governed other commodities and transactions.

**Section 14(a)** specifically required preservation for OPA examination of **invoices, sales tickets, cash receipts, and other written evidence of sale or delivery relating to the prices charged**. **Section 14(b)** required customary records of actual prices for fresh produce sold after the regulation's effective date, together with as precise a record as possible of **the basis used to determine maximum prices**. The Supreme Court reproduced the original section 14 text in [footnote 3](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/104585/shapiro-v-united-states/).

**The unit mattered as much as the dollar value.** The December 1943 version of MPR 426 expressly designed ceilings to be computed and published **in dollars and cents**, differentiated by commodity, season, grade, zone, wholesale market, and size of lot. Its lettuce schedule, for instance, specified a **$3.25 basing-point ceiling per qualifying crate plus applicable freight** for a carlot or trucklot sale; a specified smaller-lot ceiling was calculated by adding **60 cents** to that carlot/trucklot ceiling. See MPR 426 §§ 1, 8, 14–15, Appendix A, at **8 Fed. Reg. 16409–16412**. This is a documented regulatory *pricing formula*, **not a claim that Shapiro himself sold lettuce under that schedule**.

An examiner comparing a merchant's invoice with the regulation therefore had to know the product, quantity and grade, transaction date, market destination and permissible freight, any allowable markup, and the actual selling price in U.S. dollars. The regulation also prohibited evasions involving side charges, service or transportation arrangements, commissions, premiums, and tying agreements (MPR 426 § 11). A ledger entry recording only “produce — $4.00” could be inadequate to establish whether a sale complied with its particular ceiling.

For covered fresh-produce sales, the revised MPR 426 also incorporated **GMPR § 1499.12 (current records)** and **§ 1499.14 (sales slips and receipts)**, while expressly distinguishing its special price schedule from otherwise superseded rules (MPR 426 § 10, 8 Fed. Reg. 16411). **Do not presume that the general March 1942 retailer ceiling was the governing formula for every wholesale produce sale in Shapiro.**

## 5. How the required records became evidence in a criminal case

The chain of legal authority was:

1. **Congress** deemed extreme wartime price increases a national problem and delegated authority to impose fair and equitable ceilings under §§ 1–2.
2. **Congress** separately authorized compelled preservation, reports, inspection, and subpoenaed production under § 202(b).
3. **OPA** enacted commodity-specific requirements identifying the dollar ceiling and the customary transaction records and ceiling calculations that merchants must keep.
4. **Investigators** used invoices, counterparties' names, quantities, dates, and price bases to test compliance with the ceiling and anti-evasion rules.
5. **The Supreme Court** held that records lawfully required to be kept as an incident of the regulated trade could have sufficient *public aspects* that their compelled production was outside the ordinary Fifth Amendment privilege for purely private papers; the asserted statutory immunity did not bar Shapiro's prosecution. [*Shapiro*, 335 U.S. at 17–20, 32–35](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/104585/shapiro-v-united-states/).

The majority stressed that Congress had restored broad investigatory and recordkeeping powers because officials otherwise could not readily detect concealed price violations. The legislative history mattered to **both** the scope of the Administrator's authority and Congress's intended relationship between compelled records and statutory immunity. The Court relied in part on [*Wilson v. United States*, 221 U.S. 361 (1911)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1293085/wilson-v-united-states/) and [*Davis v. United States*, 328 U.S. 582 (1946)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/104313/davis-v-united-states/).

**Constitutional limit:** *Shapiro* did not decree that anything Congress orders a person to retain thereby becomes a public document. The majority recognized potential limits on required-record demands; Justice Frankfurter disputed the majority's approach in dissent. Later decisions developed limitations involving an essentially regulatory purpose, records customarily kept, and public aspects; see [*Grosso v. United States*, 390 U.S. 62, 67–68 (1968)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/107607/grosso-v-united-states/). The applicability of those criteria to other kinds of records requires a separate analysis.

## 6. What “tracking changes against U.S. dollars” accurately means

The wartime regulatory method was **nominal-dollar compliance accounting**:

- **Historical reference price:** identify the dollar amount actually charged or offered during the legally prescribed period, with product and purchaser details.
- **Legally permitted ceiling:** apply the relevant regulation, any authorized adjustment, and the correct quantity and freight basis to determine the maximum **dollars and cents per unit**.
- **Actual transaction:** preserve the contemporaneous invoice or sales slip showing the **dollar charge**, quantity, and date.
- **Change and comparison:** determine if later dollar charges or hidden extras exceeded the lawful ceiling, even if wholesale input costs increased.
- **Documentation and inspection:** retain the supporting price computation and ordinary records; submit a filing only where the specific rule required one; make records available for lawful OPA inspection.

It is a mistake to equate this with a duty to price products *relative to a foreign currency*, to compute **real** inflation-adjusted dollar values, to preserve a universal national consumer-price history, or to report every change in all prices to Congress. Congress's policy included protecting the dollar's purchasing power; the **merchant's legal obligation** was to comply with the *specific operative ceiling and records rule*. Conversely, the fact that the records were normally held by a private business did not make them immune from compulsory examination when validly required for a regulated activity.

For contrast, [*United States v. Porter*, 711 F.2d 1397, 1404–05 (7th Cir. 1983)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/421677/united-states-of-america-and-gerald-t-padar-special-agent-internal/) rejected the suggestion that **26 USC § 6001**, standing alone, transformed a taxpayer's ordinary canceled checks and deposit slips into records with the necessary public aspects. The OPA's particularized price-control and inspection regime was materially different. This comparison does not establish that all SEC-required business records are privileged or nonprivileged; that question depends on the precise scheme and records involved.
