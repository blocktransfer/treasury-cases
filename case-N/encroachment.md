# Government encroachment into private financial records

## Question presented

To what extent may Congress require financial intermediaries—and, by analogy, individuals or corporations—to create, preserve, and potentially disclose detailed records of private financial activity at their own expense in order to facilitate future government investigations? Does a record become effectively public merely because law requires its preservation?

The constitutional concern is not limited to the eventual subpoena. Requiring a private actor to build and maintain a repository of information for possible later inspection may affect financial privacy even before the government obtains the records. The governing cases nevertheless distinguish **record creation and retention**, **mandatory reporting**, and **compelled production or inspection**. A constitutional holding about one stage should not be mistaken for a holding about all three.

## California Bankers Assn. v. Shultz (1974): bank records mandated by law

**Primary authority:** [*California Bankers Assn. v. Shultz*, 416 U.S. 21 (1974)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/109005/california-bankers-assn-v-shultz/) ([opinion text](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/416/21)).

**Law challenged.** The litigation concerned the **Bank Secrecy Act of 1970** (Pub. L. 91-508), not the IRS's general taxpayer recordkeeping authority under [26 USC § 6001](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t26/s6001/). Title I empowered the Secretary of the Treasury to require banks and other financial institutions to retain identifying and transaction records, including copies of certain checks and other instruments. Title II separately authorized reporting of certain financial transactions. Congress sought records useful for criminal, tax, and regulatory investigations. The regulations addressed in the case generally prescribed **five years** of record retention, with a **two-year** period for certain demand-deposit-account reconstruction records. *Shultz*, 416 U.S. at 25–34 & n.7.

**Bank compliance costs and due process.** The Court upheld Title I's recordkeeping obligations against the banks' Fifth Amendment due-process challenge, finding an adequate relationship between the records and Congress's investigative objectives. Banks were themselves parties to the regulated transactions, had commercial reasons to maintain similar information, and could reasonably be assigned the recordkeeping task. On the evidence before it, the Court found the compliance costs not unreasonable. *Id.* at 45–50. The Court **expressly cited [*Shapiro v. United States*, 335 U.S. 1 (1948)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/104585/shapiro-v-united-states/)**, alongside *United States v. Darby*, in discussing Congress's ability to require records as an aid to legitimate regulation. *Shultz*, 416 U.S. at 46–48.

**Retention versus government access.** The majority rejected the theory that the mere creation and retention of banks' transaction records under federal regulation amounted to an unlawful Fourth Amendment seizure of depositors' papers. It emphasized that Title I did **not itself require disclosure** of the retained records to the government and that access was governed by other applicable legal process. Challenges concerning a future IRS third-party summons or compelled disclosure of particular depositors' records were not resolved absent such a concrete demand. *Id.* at 51–55. The Court also rejected the banks' Fifth Amendment self-incrimination objection because corporations do not enjoy an individual's personal privilege against compelled testimonial self-incrimination. *Id.* at 55.

**Scope of the holding.** The Court upheld a defined banking recordkeeping scheme, assessed against identified regulatory purposes and actual bank costs. It did **not** decide that Congress may compel **every individual taxpayer** to store **all potentially useful private financial records indefinitely**, or that a record is subject to unrestricted government inspection merely because a law requires its retention. Nor did it adjudicate [26 CFR § 1.6001-1(e)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/section-1.6001-1), whose retention language turns on how long contents *may become material* in tax administration.

## The dissents: the financial-privacy cost of compulsory archives

**Justice Douglas.** Dissenting in *Shultz*, Justice Douglas challenged the way mandated bank records exposed the details of customers' financial affairs. He characterized the obligation as making banks monitor and preserve information about their customers for government purposes, rather than merely administering ordinary bank transactions. The burden on banks, he argued, also supplied a concrete basis for challenging the law. *Shultz*, 416 U.S. at 79–91 (Douglas, J., dissenting). This objection concerns the **systematic creation of financial dossiers**, even when routine government access is not automatic.

**Justice Brennan.** Justice Brennan agreed with Parts I and II-A of Douglas's dissent and separately faulted Congress's **broad, indefinite delegation** of recordkeeping and reporting authority to the Treasury Secretary where constitutional rights and criminal sanctions were implicated. His objection was to the statutory scope and lack of meaningful limits, not a holding that every form of financial recordkeeping is forbidden. *Id.* at 91–94 (Brennan, J., dissenting).

These positions are dissents: the Court's controlling disposition upheld the challenged Title I bank recordkeeping provisions.

## Cross-reference: Frankfurter's “glass houses” warning in Shapiro (1948)

In [*Shapiro v. United States*, 335 U.S. 1 (1948)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/104585/shapiro-v-united-states/), the Court held that required sales records maintained under a wartime price-control regulatory scheme had public aspects sufficient to defeat the asserted Fifth Amendment objection to producing them. The majority did **not** announce that every legally required private record is automatically available to the government.

Justice Frankfurter's dissent warned against making government-mandated recordkeeping a self-executing way to strip private papers of constitutional protection:

> If records merely because required to be kept by law *ipso facto* become public records, we are indeed living in glass houses.

— *Shapiro*, **335 U.S. at 51** (Frankfurter, J., dissenting); [CourtListener opinion](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/104585/shapiro-v-united-states/).

That “glass houses” passage is from a **Supreme Court dissent**, not a majority holding. It is a direct conceptual antecedent to the privacy concerns expressed by the *Shultz* dissenters: if law can require preservation of personal financial information, the separate question is whether that requirement alone should eliminate the individual's ability to contest a later government demand for it. Importantly, the *Shultz* majority itself cited the **Shapiro majority** to support reasonable recordkeeping obligations, while retaining a distinction between **having records** and **government access to them**.

For the historical price-control setting and the particular records in *Shapiro*, see [the existing Shapiro research](../research/government-planning/shapiro-scotus.md). For *Shapiro*, the required-records doctrine, and the later distinction drawn for an individual taxpayer's records in *Porter*, see [the timeliness research](../case-1/timeliness.md).

## Outstanding questions for case-N

1. **Who must create the records?** *Shultz* placed defined retention obligations on banks transacting in interstate commerce. An obligation imposed on every individual taxpayer for ordinary personal activity presents a different factual and legal setting, even if both serve tax enforcement.
2. **For how long, and at whose expense?** The *Shultz* majority assessed actual costs under a finite retention scheme; it did not approve indefinite personal record storage on a mere possibility of future relevance. Investigate whether a particular IRS requirement has a determinate endpoint and a demonstrated administrative need.
3. **How specific is the authorization?** Compare the historical bank regulations' specified categories and periods with the general records standard in [26 USC § 6001](https://uscode.house.gov/us/usc/t26/s6001/) and [26 CFR § 1.6001-1(a), (e)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/section-1.6001-1). Identify the exact rule imposing any alleged duty before analyzing its constitutional validity.
4. **What stage is challenged?** A challenge to mandatory retention raises questions different from a challenge to a particular subpoena or disclosure. Distinguish Fourth Amendment reasonableness, Fifth Amendment due process, and an individual's testimonial privilege; corporations cannot simply assert the same Fifth Amendment privilege as their human custodians.
5. **How does the government obtain access?** *Shultz* relied in part on legally regulated access to bank records; examine actual demands and procedural protections rather than assuming that record retention itself authorizes unrestricted inspection.

**Working conclusion:** *Shultz* provides real Supreme Court authority for the government's power to impose **reasonable, finite financial recordkeeping duties on banks**. Its reasoning and the dissents expose competing constitutional interests, but do not supply a categorical answer about **open-ended IRS record retention imposed on ordinary individuals or corporations**. The question must be framed around the specified duty, the regulated party, costs, duration, purposes, and manner of government access.
